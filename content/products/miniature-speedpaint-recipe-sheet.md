## PAGE 2 — The method  
  
## The whole thing in one line  
  
**Prime black → heavy grey drybrush → light white drybrush → one coat of contrast paint → spot highlight → base.**  
This is slapchop. The two drybrush passes build a light map by hand, so the translucent paint that follows lands with shading already underneath it. That is the entire trick, and it is why this needs no airbrush.  
## The six steps

|  |  |  |  |  |

|  |  |  |  |  |
|  |  |  |  |  |
|  |  |  |  |  |
|  |  |  |  |  |
  
**Where the time goes: step 4.** Everything else exists to make step 4 do two jobs at once — colour and shading in a single pass. That is the saving.  
## The three rules  
  
1. **One coat of contrast paint. Never two.** A second coat floods the recesses and kills the gradient the first coat made. If the colour looks thin, you wanted a different colour, not more of this one.  
2. **Do not thin contrast paints like normal paint.** They are formulated to flow. Thinning them makes them a wash, and a wash is a different product.  
3. **Be mean with the white.** More white does not mean more contrast. It means a chalky model with no dark left in it.  
## What this method will not do  
  
It will not produce blends, glazes, or object-source lighting. It is not supposed to. Use it on the other forty models so you have the evenings free for the one you actually want to fuss over.

## 6 — PALE AND ETHEREAL  
  
*Ghosts, spirits, anything that should look lit from inside.*  
**⚠ This recipe breaks the method — read this before starting.**  
Prime **white or light grey**, not black. Skip the grey drybrush. Do a light grey drybrush *into the recesses* instead of a white drybrush onto the edges. Then one coat of a bright, thin contrast — turquoise, pale blue or green.

## 7 — BLACK ARMOUR  
  
*The hard one. Black is not the absence of colour and it is not easy.*  
**Do not use a black contrast paint over the slapchop base and expect it to work.** It floods everything, kills the grey map, and produces a flat dark smudge. Black armour needs a different approach.

## PAGE 6 — Basing and batching  
  
## Basing, in four moves  
  
1. **Texture** — paste or PVA-and-sand across the base, avoiding the model's feet.  
2. **Drybrush one shade lighter** than the texture. Not two shades. One.  
3. **Two or three tufts, placed off-centre.** Never in the middle, never evenly spaced, never one in each corner.  
4. **Rim the base edge last**, in a single flat colour, with a loaded brush held sideways.  
**Basing is the highest ratio of perceived finish to time spent in this entire sheet.** A tabletop model on a bare black base looks unfinished. The same model on textured ground with two tufts reads as done. It costs two minutes.  
## Batch the unit, do not finish models  
  
Do **one step across the whole unit**, never one model to completion.  
Prime ten. Drybrush ten. Drybrush ten again. Colour ten. Base ten.  
This is what makes this an army method rather than a model method. Two reasons, and the second is the important one:  
* You stop reloading the same paint and rinsing the same brush ten times.  
* **Your models match.** Colour matching drifts across a session. If you paint model one on Tuesday and model ten a fortnight later, they will not be the same green, and a mismatched squad is far more obvious on the table than a slightly rough highlight.  
## Write the recipe down as you go  
  
Record the **exact** paint names while the pots are still on the desk. Months later, when the army expands, you will not remember them. Page 8 is for this.  
## Test one model first  
  
Paint **one** standalone model before committing the unit. It costs one model to find out a scheme does not work. It costs an army to find out later.  
  
## PAGE 7 — When it goes wrong  
  
## 1. "It looks chalky and washed out."  
  
**Too much white drybrush.** The most common failure by a distance. The white pass is for edges and topmost points, not for the model. Fix: another coat of contrast over the affected area will pull the value back down. Prevention: wipe the brush until you think there is no paint left, then wipe it once more.  
## 2. "The colour went blotchy and patchy."  
  
You went back over a panel that was half dry. Contrast paints reactivate and lift. Fix: one further thin coat across the **whole** panel, laid in one pass, no revisiting. Prevention: load the brush properly, work one panel start to finish, move on.  
## 3. "It pooled in the wrong places and looks dirty."  
  
Too much paint on the brush, and the model was probably held flat. Fix: while still wet, wick the excess off with a clean dry brush. Prevention: touch the brush to a paper towel before it touches the model, and turn the model as you work so gravity pulls paint toward the recesses rather than into flat panels.  
## 4. "The recesses aren't dark enough."  
  
Your grey drybrush was too heavy and got into the recesses. Fix: a thin dark wash into the recesses only. Prevention: less paint, lighter pressure, and drybrush *across* the detail rather than into it.  
## 5. "It looks flat and boring."  
  
Almost always missing step 5. Ten minutes of edge highlighting on the few edges that catch the light will change the model more than any other ten minutes you can spend. If highlighting has not fixed it, the scheme itself is too low-contrast — see recipe 7 for the fix, which is to add contrast from the base and the metal rather than from the armour.  
## The two-minute check  
  
Put the model on the table. Stand up. Look at it from where you actually sit when playing. **Everything you cannot see from there does not matter, and everything you can see from there is the whole product.** Most people paint detail nobody will ever look at and skip the base, which everybody sees.
