## PAGE 2 — THE CARD  
  
**START HERE, EVERY TIME**  
  
```
DOSE     18 g in           ← lock this. Do not move it.
YIELD    36 g out          ← 1:2 ratio
TIME     25–32 s           ← from the moment the pump starts
TEMP     ~93°C / 200°F     ← if your machine lets you set it

```
  
**THE ORDER — THIS IS THE WHOLE CARD**  
  
**1. Lock the dose.**  
  
**2. Move the grind until the TIME is right.**  
  
**3. Only then judge the taste.**  
  
**Change one thing at a time.** Move grind and dose together and the result tells you nothing — you have spent the coffee and learned nothing from it.  
**WHAT IT TASTES LIKE → WHAT TO DO**  
  

| It tastes | That means | Do this |
| ------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| SOUR — sharp, thin, empty, disappears fast | Under-extracted. Water went through too quickly | GRIND FINER. One step. Pull again |
| BITTER — harsh, dry, lingers unpleasantly | Over-extracted. Water went through too slowly, or too hot, or the machine is dirty | GRIND COARSER. One step. Pull again |
| SOUR AND BITTER TOGETHER | ⚠ CHANNELLING. This is not a grind problem | ⚠ DO NOT TOUCH THE GRINDER. Fix your puck prep — see below |
| WATERY, PALE, NO BODY | Ratio too wide, or the dose is too low | Cut the yield back toward 1:2, or check you are actually dosing what you think |
| HARSH AND ASHY even at the right time | Too hot, or an unclean machine | Drop the temperature. Then clean the group head and the basket |
  
**⚠ THE ONE PEOPLE GET WRONG**  
  
**Sour and bitter at the same time is not "somewhere in the middle". It is channelling.**  
Water found a low-resistance path through the puck. Part of the coffee over-extracted while the rest barely brewed, and you are tasting both at once.  
**Grinding finer to chase the sourness makes it worse.** It raises the pressure against a puck that already has a hole in it.  
**Fix the puck, not the grind:**  
1. **WDT** — stir the grounds with fine needles. Break every clump.  
2. **Level the bed** before you tamp.  
3. **Tamp LEVEL.** Level matters far more than hard.  
4. Only if it still channels: one step finer, to raise puck density.  
**ONE STEP. RE-PULL. ONE STEP. RE-PULL.**  
  
Two changes at once and you have no information.  
  
## PAGE 3 — The order, and why it is the order  
  
**The order again, with the reasoning**  
  
**1. Lock the dose.**  
Your dose is a property of your basket, not of the coffee. An 18 g basket wants roughly 18 g. Once you have found what your basket takes, it stops being a variable and becomes a constant. Writing it down and never touching it again removes an entire dimension from the problem.  
**Why this comes first:** dose changes puck depth, which changes resistance, which changes shot time — so it moves the same needle grind does. If both are moving, you cannot attribute a change to either.  
**2. Move the grind until the time is right.**  
Grind is your resistance dial and it is the only one you should be turning during a dial-in. Finer means slower, coarser means faster. Adjust until an 18 g dose gives you 36 g in 25–32 seconds.  
**Why time before taste:** a shot pulled far outside the time window tastes bad for structural reasons that have nothing to do with the coffee. Tasting a 14-second shot to decide whether you like the beans is a wasted cup. Get the shot into the window first, then the taste becomes information.  
**3. Only then judge the taste, and adjust the yield.**  
Now the taste is telling you about extraction rather than about a broken shot. Widen the ratio toward 1:2.5 for a lighter, sweeter, more delicate cup; tighten toward 1:1.5 for something heavier and more intense.  
**Why beginners are told the opposite, and why they are not wrong**  
  
Most beginner guidance says "taste it, then move the grinder". That is not bad advice — it assumes your dose is already fixed, which for someone using one basket and a set scoop it usually is.  
**This card uses the professional ordering because at the start your dose is usually *not* fixed**, and that is exactly why beginners' dial-ins wander for weeks without converging. Once your dose is genuinely locked, the two approaches are the same thing.  
**The ratio, plainly**  
  
**1:2 is the starting point.** 18 g in, 36 g out. Modern specialty practice runs roughly **1:2 to 1:2.5**.  
Start at 1:2 and only widen once the shot is clean. A wide ratio on a channelling shot just gives you more of a bad thing.  
**One variable. Then re-pull.**  
  
This is the single rule that separates a dial-in that converges in four shots from one that never converges at all. It feels slow. It is not — it is the fast way, because every shot produces usable information.  
  
## PAGE 4 — The decision tree  
  
```
PULL A SHOT. WEIGH IT. TIME IT. TASTE IT.
│
├─ SOUR AND BITTER TOGETHER?
│    └─ ⚠ CHANNELLING — do not touch the grinder
│         1. WDT — stir with fine needles, break every clump
│         2. Level the bed
│         3. Tamp level, not hard
│         4. Still channelling? ONE step finer
│       → Re-pull before you change anything else
│
├─ ONLY SOUR?
│    ├─ Time under ~25 s?  → GRIND FINER, one step
│    ├─ Time in range?     → Raise the temperature slightly
│    └─ Still sour?        → Extend the yield toward 1:2.5
│
├─ ONLY BITTER?
│    ├─ Time over ~32 s?   → GRIND COARSER, one step
│    ├─ Time in range?     → Lower the temperature slightly
│    └─ Still bitter?      → CLEAN the group head and basket, re-pull
│
├─ WATERY / THIN, but balanced?
│    └─ Cut the yield toward 1:2
│       or raise the dose within what the basket takes
│
├─ CHOKING — barely any flow?
│    └─ Far too fine. Go SEVERAL steps coarser, not one
│
├─ GUSHING — done in under 15 s?
│    └─ Far too coarse, OR the puck cracked
│       Check prep first, then go several steps finer
│
└─ TASTES GOOD?
     └─ WRITE IT DOWN. Page 6. You will not remember it.

```
  
**Reading the tree**  
  
**"One step" means one step on your grinder** — whatever the smallest meaningful adjustment on your equipment is. On a stepped grinder that is one click. On a stepless one it is a small, repeatable nudge you can find again.  
**"Several steps" is for the two broken cases only** — choking and gushing. Everywhere else, one step.  
  
## PAGE 5 — Puck prep, and temperature  
  
**Puck prep — the part that fixes most problems**  
  
Most dial-in problems that survive three grind adjustments are not grind problems. They are prep problems.  
**1. WDT — stir the grounds with fine needles.**  
Breaks up the clumps that come off any grinder and evens out the density across the basket. **This is the single highest-value habit on this card.** It costs ten seconds and it is the difference between a puck that extracts evenly and one that has a channel waiting in it.  
**2. Level the bed before you tamp.**  
A tilted or uneven bed becomes a tilted puck, and water goes through the shallow side.  
**3. Tamp level, not hard.**  
**Levelness matters far more than force.** Chasing tamp pressure is wasted effort — past the point where the puck is compacted, more force achieves nothing. A tilted tamp, however, guarantees a channel.  
**The three causes of channelling**  
  
Clumps, a tilted tamp, and uneven distribution. **All three are prep. None of them are grind.** That is the whole reason the sour-and-bitter case sends you here instead of to the grinder.  
**Temperature**  
  
* Typical brew range is roughly **90–96°C / 195–205°F**.  
* **~93°C / 200°F is the default start.**  
* **Lighter roast → go hotter.** Light roasts are denser and harder to extract.  
* **Darker roast → go cooler.** Dark roasts give up their solubles easily and turn bitter and ashy when pushed.  
**If your machine has no temperature control, ignore this section entirely.** That is most consumer machines, and it is not a problem — grind and ratio are the two dials that matter most, and you have both.  
**Fresh beans move under you**  
  
A bean's dial-in **drifts over the days after roast**. Coffee that is very fresh degasses and behaves differently from the same bag a fortnight later.  
**If a setting that worked last week is sour today, the coffee changed, not your technique.** Adjust and move on. This is normal and it is not a fault in your process.  
**One workaround, flagged as exactly what it is**  
  
If a shot is technically dialled in and *still* tastes slightly sour, a trace of sodium bicarbonate in the cup will neutralise residual acidity.  
**This is a correction at the cup, not a fix to the shot.** It changes what you are drinking, not what your machine did. Use it knowing that, and do not use it to paper over a shot you have not actually dialled in.  
  
## PAGE 6 — Log sheet, and what is general vs yours  
  
**General — applies on any machine, anywhere**  
  
* The 1:2 ratio and the 25–32 second window as a starting point  
* The sour/bitter/both logic  
* The adjustment order: dose, then grind to time, then yield  
* Everything on the puck prep page  
* Temperature direction by roast level  
* Fresh beans drifting  
**Specific to your machine — never copy these from anyone**  
  
* **Grinder settings and click counts.** Completely meaningless between grinders. Someone else's "12 clicks" tells you absolutely nothing about your grinder, even the same model, even the same bag of coffee.  
* **Pressure profiling.** Needs a machine that can do it. Most cannot.  
* **"9 bar."** Fixed and not user-adjustable on most consumer machines. It is not a dial you have, and reading about it will not help you.  
* **Your dose range.** A property of your basket. Find it once, write it down.  
**The rule: if a number came from someone else's machine, it is a starting point at best and noise at worst.**
